UX / UI · Accessibility · 2026

Adaptive Legibility for iOS 26

An Apple-style concept feature that fixes Liquid Glass readability by adapting blur, opacity and tint to the wallpaper and the light around you, lifting contrast from 2.0:1 to WCAG AA 4.5:1.

FigmaInteraction designWCAGiOS
View prototype
2.0 → 4.5contrast ratio
<16 msadaptation time
7 / 7accessibility checks

The problem

iOS 26’s Liquid Glass looks beautiful, but on colourful wallpapers, bright gradients or in direct sunlight its translucent panels drop to 1.5–2.0:1 contrast, far below the 4.5:1 WCAG AA minimum. That turns into a real barrier for people with low vision, colour blindness or high cognitive load.

Existing options fall short

I compared the three current approaches on the same wallpaper. Standard glass fails at 2.0:1, Tinted Mode reaches only 3.7:1, and Reduce Transparency hits 4.2:1 but loses the Liquid Glass look entirely.

The solution

Adaptive Legibility detects the environment with the light sensor, analyses wallpaper brightness and colour complexity on-device, then tunes blur, opacity and tint in a single frame. It respects accessibility settings, raising contrast further for low-vision users, and lives in Settings with per-environment controls.

Outcome

A 26-screen clickable Figma prototype that walks through the problem, current options, how the feature works and live scenarios, ending at 4.5:1 contrast while keeping the Liquid Glass aesthetic.

UX research · 2026

Amazon Checkout: A Heuristic Evaluation

A screen-by-screen audit of Amazon.ca’s checkout against Nielsen’s 10 heuristics: 8 issues rated for severity, 3 redesign recommendations.

Heuristic evaluationNielsen’s 10RedesignDGM 6168
Read case study
8issues found
4screens audited
3recommendations

Approach

I walked through the live desktop checkout as a repeat shopper (cart, secure checkout, review and confirmation), captured annotated screenshots at every step, and rated each issue on Nielsen’s 0–4 severity scale. I also recorded what Amazon gets right, like opt-in add-ons and a clear, code-free card error, so the ratings reflect the screen and not a bias toward fault-finding.

Key findings

  • Major: no visible way to cancel right after placing an order; the path is buried in Your Orders.
  • Minor: “Eligible for FREE Shipping” in the cart, then a $9.87 shipping charge at review.
  • Minor: credit card and Prime promotions crowd the checkout action; an expired card stays selectable.

Recommendations

Subordinate promotions to the task, show the real cost where the user needs it, and add a visible “Cancel this order” link on the confirmation page.

Dior: Content Strategy & Brand Voice

A two-part study of how Dior builds luxury online: a content analysis across eight platforms, then a brand voice and messaging framework with platform-specific tone and community replies.

Content analysisBrand voiceMessaging pillarsCommunity management
Content analysis Brand voice deck
8platforms mapped
3voice traits

Made with Kamakshi, Prakriti and Yellow.

Part 1: Content analysis

We defined Dior’s positioning, then checked whether its content delivers on it. Dior communicates three ideas: heritage made contemporary, femininity as strength, and fashion as self-expression. It does this through campaign films, runway coverage, atelier craftsmanship stories, ambassadors and cultural programming.

  • Platform ecosystem: mapped the role of Instagram (about 47M followers on the main account), YouTube, TikTok, Snapchat AR try-ons, Pinterest, LinkedIn, Facebook/X and podcasts.
  • Content roles: sorted content by whether it informs, engages, inspires or converts.
  • Verdict: strong alignment between promise and execution. Dior sells identity, not just inventory.

Part 2: Brand voice and messaging

  • Voice traits: elegant, confident, bold.
  • Messaging pillars: femininity with strength, luxury with craftsmanship, dreamlike lifestyle, personal expression, heritage with modern culture.
  • Tone by platform: wrote the same idea three ways, polished for LinkedIn, playful for TikTok and cinematic for campaign film, to show different tones with one identity.
  • Community engagement: drafted on-brand replies to real Reddit posts asking for recommendations, complaining about pigment, and discussing products, turning criticism into helpful, honest guidance.

Wellness Flower

JavaScriptD3 / SVGPersonal data
Live demo GitHub

Hypothesis: higher daily screen time goes with higher stress. I tracked 30 consecutive days (Oct 8 to Nov 6, 2025) of screen time, social media, school work, sleep, and self-rated stress and productivity.

  • Each day is a petal: angle shows screen time, distance from center shows sleep, color shows stress, size shows productivity.

  • Finding: past about eight hours of screen time, stress jumps and sleep and productivity drop, with sleep playing a key mediating role.

Information & memory

From the Memex to NotebookLM

A reflection on Vannevar Bush’s 1945 “As We May Think”: how his vision of technology that extends human memory lives on in tools like NotebookLM, and where hands-free AR interaction might take it next.

Vannevar BushAI toolsAR interaction
3 min readRead reflection

Vannevar Bush wrote this essay in 1945, near the end of World War II, when scientific research and technology were developing very quickly. He wants to use the knowledge that already existed so that people could access, organize and connect them to create more information.

Bush’s idea about the relationship between “thinking man and the sum of our knowledge” is interesting because he recognizes that human memory have limitations but he also sees human as creators and users of knowledge. We can see different technologies developed today based on his ideas to store the data of different sectors where we can store all the information and can be access whenever we need it.

Bush’s idea about the memex became important because he imagined personal device that could store books, records, communications, notes, photographs and other information. To me, this shows that Bush did not imagine technology simple replacing human thinking instead he imagined technology extending human memory and helping make connections.

One of Bush’s speculations was that information technology would be possible to store amount of information and retrieve it through different medium. He was directly involved in scientific research during World War II where he had direct experience with both the power of scientific collaboration and the problem of managing huge amount of research from where he believed scientists should use the new technologies for making human knowledge more accessible.

Based on that one example that relates strongly to Bush’s ideas is Google NotebookLM which was released in 2024 which allows users to upload sources such as PDFs, Google Docs, slides, websites, YouTube videos and audio files and then interact with those sources using AI which can summarize information, answer questions, create study guides, and make connections between different sources. I see a strong connection between NotebookLM and Bush’s Memex where Bush imagined a personal information system where a person could keep a large collection of knowledge and move between connected pieces of information where a NotebookLM does something similar in a modern way.

My own speculation is that future technology will move even further from simply storing information. For example, the technology allow people to interact with information without physically touching the device. For example, as we discussed in class to about hand free interactions where future AR glasses could use cameras and sensors to track a person’s eyes, hand gestures, and voice which we can use it to track a document, hand gesture to save it and connect it to another document simply by moving their hands. This connects to our class discussion about free hand interaction devices because it shows how technology could move away from keyboards and touchscreens.

In a class discussion different points were raised related to Bush’s idea which I totally agree with because in part of my analysis I also found the same because I can connect class discussion with the concept of NotebookLM and hand free interactions. As we also talk about future concepts of AI how AI can bring the future concept of accessing the data storing the information and the movement of hand gestures leading to make an robots which can be programmed as human doing artificial works by on their own.

References

Bush, V. (1945). As we may think. The Atlantic.

Access & equity

People + Technology = Possibilities

Comparing Barlow’s Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace with AI democratization, Free Basics and One Laptop per Child, and what my own school years in Nepal taught me about access.

DemocratizationEthicsNepal
4 min readRead reflection

To me, democratization of technology means letting more people use technology so they have a chance to learn and share their ideas. In this reflection, I compare two cases: the Declaration of Independence of Cyberspace and AI democratization.

In 1996, John Perry Barlow wrote “A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace” because he was upset with the U.S. Telecommunications Act, which tried to control what people could say online. He wanted the internet to stay free from the government. He wanted anyone to be able to share their ideas, and he believed everyone should be equal, no matter how much money they have or where they come from. In my view, his dream is not really possible today. Most social media platforms have to follow the laws of the country where they work. Every country has its own laws, beliefs, and values, so the internet is still affected by governments.

AI democratization is about giving more people access to new technology. AI can help people learn, work, and make new things. But not everyone has the same access because some people do not have enough money, good internet, or the skills to use it. Nowadays, many students in Nepal can easily use ChatGPT. But many of them do not know how to use it in the right way for learning or for building their careers.

I think both cases are similar because they are both about giving more people access to technology. The Declaration is more about freedom on the internet, while AI democratization is more about giving people access to AI. Both show me that technology can give people more chances, but there can still be problems with access and control.

In my opinion, Free Basics mostly helped Facebook more than the people. Social media is a place where people share their ideas with the world. I think what people share should be open for everyone to see, instead of being inside Facebook’s small and controlled space.

I think a company can help people and make money at the same time. Making money is not always bad because a company needs money to keep working and help more people. But I do not think it is fair when a company says it is helping people only to get more power or control. For me, a fair company should help people and also give them choices. People should not feel like they have to use only one company. Making money is okay when the company is honest and people also get something good from it.

In our in-class activity, we talked about the One Laptop per Child program. Our class felt that it failed because it gave children laptops, but it did not give them enough knowledge or teachers to help them use the laptops.

Until class 6, my school in Nepal did not have computers. Because of this, I changed to another school that had enough computers. There, my teachers taught me computer basics and QBasic programming. I learned how to make simple patterns like star shapes and ladder shapes. My own life is an example of People + Technology = Possibilities.

My old schoolmates were the same kind of students as me, but they did not get the same chance to use computers. Because of this, they had fewer chances to learn these skills, and they fell behind.

In the end, I learned that giving people technology is not enough. People also need teachers, knowledge, and help to use it. Technology can give people more chances, but not everyone gets the same chance. From these examples and my own life, I learned that true democratization is not only about giving people technology. It is also about helping people learn how to use it. When people have both technology and support, they can learn more and have a better future.

References

Barlow, J. P. (1996). A declaration of the independence of cyberspace. Electronic Frontier Foundation.

Solon, O. (2017, July 27). The Guardian.

Design ethics

Designing with Empathy

McDonagh and Thomas on empathy-driven design versus Robert Yang’s critique of “empathy machines,” applied to my Himalayan hut VR escape room and how to represent Nepal respectfully.

Design thinkingVREmpathy
4 min readRead reflection

In “Rethinking Design Thinking: Empathy Supporting Innovation,” McDonagh and Thomas talk about how empathy helps designers understand people and make better things. In “Empathy Machines as Appropriation Machines,” Robert Yang explains how games and VR can make people think they understand others when they do not know their full story. To me, designing with empathy means thinking about what other people need and making things that help them. I think it is more about understanding people first, not just making them feel something. My way of designing is a mix of both authors’ ideas.

Both authors agree that designers should care about real people and their lives. McDonagh and Thomas (2012) explain that designers should watch and listen to people and talk with them to understand what they need (p. 460). This is better than just guessing what people want. Yang has a different view about games and VR. He says, “At best, VR can only offer the illusion of empathy” (Yang, 2017). He worries that players may think they understand someone’s life when they do not know the full story. He also says, “A better empathy should center people, not machines or industries or brands” (Yang, 2017). The main difference is that McDonagh and Thomas focus on understanding people to make better designs, while Yang worries that empathy games may make players feel something without really helping others. I think both readings are useful because design should help people, but we should not think a game can teach us everything about another person’s life.

I think McDonagh and Thomas support empathy because they want designers to make things that help people in real life. When designers understand what people need, they can make products that are easier to use and more helpful. They also explain that designers can build trust by having informal conversations with people (McDonagh & Thomas, 2012, p. 460). I think this is important because people may share more when they feel comfortable. Yang is worried because a game may make the player feel sad or happy without helping the person whose story is being told. I think real people should get a chance to share their own stories so others can learn from them.

The idea “People + Technology = Possibilities” connects to both readings. For McDonagh and Thomas, people and technology can work together to make better things that help users. For Yang, technology like VR can help people learn, but it can also make them think they understand someone’s life when they do not. I think technology can be useful if we use it in the right way. My Himalayan hut VR game is an escape room made in ShapesXR. In the game, a blizzard traps a climber inside a mountain hut. The player needs to solve puzzles, fix a broken radio, and call a rescue helicopter before it gets dark. I want players to have fun and also learn about the mountains of Nepal. I want to include real things like prayer flags and mountain life. I can use my own knowledge of Nepal, but I should also do research and learn from people who live in the mountains. If I am not sure about something, I will check good sources or ask people who know more about it. I want my game to show Nepal and its culture in a true and respectful way. This also connects to Yang’s idea that empathy should put real people first, not just the game or the people who make it.

When I think about both readings, I agree with parts of each one. I agree with McDonagh and Thomas because design should help people and make things easy for them. For example, in my website class project, I need to make a website that is simple so people can find things easily. I also agree with Yang because we cannot fully know how other people feel just by playing a game. We are not living their real life. However, I think Yang is a little too negative about empathy in games because games can still help people learn about others. In my Himalayan hut VR game, I know players cannot learn everything about mountain life from one game, but they can still learn something new.

In the end, I think empathy is important in design, but we should use it in the right way. We should listen to real people and not think we know everything about them. A game or a design can help people learn, but it cannot show everything about real life. Good design should help people and respect their stories.

References

McDonagh, D., & Thomas, J. (2012). Rethinking design thinking: Empathy supporting innovation. Australasian Medical Journal, 3(8), 458–464.

Yang, R. (2017, April 6). Empathy machines as appropriation machines. Radiator Blog.
